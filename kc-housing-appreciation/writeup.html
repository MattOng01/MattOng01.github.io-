Overview

Do cheaper neighborhoods catch up to expensive ones over time, or does the gap between them persist — or even widen? This project tests that question in Kansas City housing markets using two related but distinct convergence concepts from Growth Economics, going back to Robert Solow's 1956 growth model: Beta Convergence, which asks whether lower-priced ZIP-codes appreciate faster than higher-priced ones, and Sigma Convergence, which asks whether the overall spread between rich and poor ZIP codes is shrinking over time.

The distinction matters beyond the theory. If cheaper ZIP codes are systematically catching up, that has real implications for where affordability pressure is building or easing, where investment capital is likely to flow next, and how urban planners and policymakers should think about equitable growth across a metro area.

Using ZIP-code-level home price data for the Kansas City MSA from 2000–2026, this project finds evidence of both beta and sigma convergence. However, as the sections below show, the strength of that convergence varies considerably depending on the time period examined and isn't immune to reversal during periods of economic stress.

Beta Convergence

What It Is and Why It Matters

Growth Economics theorizes that economies with different levels of growth will experience different rates of growth. Robert Solow (1956) predicted that less-developed economies will develop faster than more advanced economics as they approach their long-run level of wealth. This conclusion comes from the relative marginal productivity of inputs.

A similar phenomenon occurs in real estate markets when lower-priced markets appreciate faster than higher priced markets. This phenomenon, known as Beta convergence (β-convergence), has significant implications for policy and investment.

The regression specification follows the standard formulation:

\[ \log\!\left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,0}}\right) = \alpha + \beta\,\log(P_{i,0}) + \varepsilon_i \]

where \(P_{i,0}\) is the median home value in ZIP code \(i\) at the start of the study period and \(P_{i,t}\) is the value at the end. A negative coefficient on initial price implies that price gaps between cheap and expensive ZIP codes are narrowing over time, consistent with capital flowing toward undervalued markets, demand spillovers from expensive neighborhoods into adjacent affordable ones, and supply constraints binding more tightly in already-expensive areas.

Data

Home value data are drawn from the Zillow Home Value Index (ZHVI) at the ZIP-Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) level. Each year’s data point is January’s average home sales. The data was filtered to only include the 200 ZIP-codes in the Kansas City MSA. This public dataset covers home prices from January 2000 until January 2026. However, the dataset has significant gaps. Zillow coverage thins considerably for earlier periods. My first step was identifying what ZIP-codes were missing across the data and, importantly, determining when the dataset was complete enough for meaningful results.

I found the number of missing ZIP-Codes for each year and calculated the percent of missing ZIP-Codes.

The period 2001-2026 includes all possible years but has the most missing data. The first year where over 90 percent of ZIP-codes report data is 2012. Data coverage improves again in 2018 and 2020.

To better understand what ZIP-codes are missing, I mapped each of those periods, where ZIP-codes in red are missing from the sample. Exclusions do not appear to be random and are discussed under Limitations.

Results and Discussion

All four study periods show a statistically significant negative coefficient on initial log home price, providing consistent evidence of beta convergence across every window tested. The magnitude of the effect, however, is not stable over time. The 2012–2026 period shows the strongest convergence signal (β = -0.368), nearly 2 times larger than the other three windows. This is consistent with the economic narrative underlying the period selection: 2012 marks the start of the post-Financial Crisis recovery, when lower-priced ZIP-codes, disproportionately affected most by foreclosure, had the most room to recover relative to already-expensive markets that held their value through the downturn.

The shorter, more recent windows (2018–2026, 2020–2026) show progressively weaker convergence (β = -0.230 and -0.161, respectively), alongside declining R2 (0.708 and 0.571). This pattern suggests that as the Financial Crisis recovery matured, the convergence weakened.

This seems plausible, as the Pandemic-era housing market was driven by remote work migration, inventory shortages, historically low interest rates, and many people paying cash for homes – something we would expect more of in affluent neighborhoods.

The 2001–2026 full-cycle estimate (β = -0.237) falls between these, but should be interpreted cautiously given it reflects only 62 of 200 ZIPs (see Limitations.).

Given this pattern, the 2012–2026 period was selected as the primary specification for further analysis, both because it shows the strongest and best-fitting convergence relationship (R2 = 0.796) and because it spans a coherent, economically meaningful regime rather than a shorter or more volatile window.

Spatial Autocorrelation

The statistical test used to calculate beta convergence (OLS regression) depends on the assumption of independent statistical errors across observations. Given that home price appreciation is inherently geographic, this assumption is unlikely to hold: appreciation in one ZIP-code plausibly correlates with appreciation in adjacent ZIP-codes. This may be due to direct spillover (demand or development effects crossing ZIP-code boundaries) or shared unobserved factors (school quality, walkability, amenity access) that themselves cluster spatially.

For example, Shawnee Mission East, a premier public high school, is located in 66208; however, ZIP-code 66207 is still within district boundaries. Families that were drawn to Shawnee Mission East were not limited to just homes in 66208. This is an example of a spill-over effect, and would violate the independent error assumptions for OLS and is known as Spatial Autocorrelation.

Luckily, there is a method to test for and address this violation. Spatial autocorrelation was tested for the primary 2012–2026 specification.

Weights matrix. First, we assign a weight to each ZIP-code. There are several ways to do this, and the method depends on the type of spatial autocorrelation we suspect. In this case, I expect most of the autocorrelation to be spill-over effects (such as in the Shawnee Mission East example). Therefore, my weight matrix followed a Queen Contiguity pattern. In this type of matrix, the weight assigned to a ZIP-code depends on the number of direct neighboring ZIP-codes it has, and the rows are standardized so that each ZIP's neighbors are weighted equally and sum to one. No islands (zero-neighbor ZIPs) were present. The average ZIP had approximately 5.2 neighbors. The full spatial weights matrix is available in the project repository. Sigma Convergence

What It Is and Why It Matters

Sigma convergence (σ-convergence) measures whether the cross-sectional dispersion, or the range of home values across ZIP codes is declining over time. Where beta convergence tests the rate relationship between initial price and subsequent growth, sigma convergence directly tracks whether the distribution of prices is compressing. Formally, sigma convergence is present when: \[ \sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\log P_{i,t} - \overline{\log P_t}\right)^2} \] is declining over time. Beta convergence is a necessary but not sufficient condition for sigma convergence.

Data

Annual \(\sigma_t\) is computed for each year 2000–2026 using all ZIPs with non-missing Zillow ZHVI values in that year. The full time series is plotted below.

Figure 5. Cross-sectional standard deviation of log home values across KC MSA ZIP codes, 2000–2026. Shading indicates four identified convergence regimes.

The graph reveals four sub-periods were used to explore Sigma Convergence. Again, these sub-periods are economically significant:

  • 2000–2026: The full model.
  • 2000–2006: Pre-Financial Crisis expansion, where broad appreciation was driven by subprime credit expansion. Negative slope, indicating price compression.
  • 2007–2014: The Financial Crisis and aftermath. Foreclosure crisis differentially compressed lower-tier markets, widening dispersion – positive slope.
  • 2015–2026: Post-recovery through the Pandemic. Sustained compression as demand shifted from expensive to affordable ZIPs – negative slope, again.

Analysis

Linear time trends are estimated within each sub-period to characterize the direction and pace of dispersion change:

sigma2000_2026 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = sigma_data)
sigma2000_2006 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2000, Year <= 2006))
sigma2007_2014 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2007, Year <= 2014))
sigma2015_2026 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2015))

Figure 6. Cross-sectional dispersion with fitted linear trends overlaid for each sub-period. Positive slope = divergence; negative slope = sigma convergence.

Discussion

The sigma convergence results tell a more cyclical story than beta convergence alone would suggest. The range of log home values compressed steadily during the pre-Financial Crisis expansion (2000–2006), consistent with broad-based appreciation lifting lower-value ZIPs alongside higher-value ones.

This compression reversed sharply during the Financial Crisis and its aftermath (2007–2014), when dispersion widened. This is consistent with the foreclosure crisis disproportionately depressing lower-income markets rather than affecting all ZIP-codes uniformly, temporarily undoing prior convergence gains. From 2015 onward, dispersion resumed its decline, consistent with the strong housing market and beta convergence signal identified in the 2012–2026 regression.

This regime-dependent pattern is a useful complement to the beta convergence findings: it demonstrates that convergence is not a constant process, but can instead pause or reverse during periods of asymmetric shock. It also reinforces that beta convergence alone is an incomplete test. While a negative beta coefficient indicates a tendency toward compression on average, the sigma convergence series shows this tendency was actively overridden during 2007–2014, precisely the kind of divergence episode beta convergence alone cannot capture.

Limitations

  • Survivorship bias. ZIPs excluded from earlier periods due to missing Zillow coverage are disproportionately low-value and low-turnover markets — the same ZIPs most likely to be driving the convergence result. Estimates for earlier periods likely understate the true rate of convergence.
  • ZCTA vs. ZIP codes. Zillow reports at the ZIP code level; Census boundaries use ZCTAs, which do not always align exactly with USPS ZIP codes. Boundary discrepancies are minor but not zero.
  • OLS assumptions. Standard OLS convergence regressions assume independent errors. Spatial autocorrelation testing (see above) evaluates whether this assumption holds; results should be interpreted accordingly.
  • OLS assumptions, cont.OLS also requires that no there are no ommitted variables. There are obviously spatial and non-spatial variables that will impact appreciation rates. This is strictly a unconditional convergence test. No casual inference should be drawn.
  • Single metro. Findings are specific to the Kansas City MSA and may not generalize to other metro areas with different supply elasticities, demographic compositions, or spatial structures.
