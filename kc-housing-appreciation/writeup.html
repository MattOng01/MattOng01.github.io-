Overview

This project looks at two growth economics phenomena: beta and sigma convergence.

One theory of economic growth, formalized by Robert Solow in 1956, illustrates how economies grow at different rates depending on their level of economic output. In the long run, Solow argued, economies converge at a stable level of output, so more affluent economies would grow relatively slower as they approach this level compared to less affluent economies.

This has important implications for real estate, urban planning, and policy.

This growth convergence has two related but different identifiers, called beta and sigma convergence.

Beta convergence occurs when less affluent economies grow faster than more affluent economies.

Sigma convergence occurs when the gap between rich and poor economies shrinks — the standard deviation of their log growth becomes smaller across time.

In Kansas City, there is evidence of both beta and sigma convergence in ZIP-code level house prices from 2000–2026.

Beta Convergence

What It Is and Why It Matters

Beta convergence (β-convergence) tests whether lower-priced markets appreciate faster than higher-priced ones — the housing analog of the Barro and Sala-i-Martin (1992) convergence hypothesis from growth economics. A negative coefficient on initial price implies that price gaps between cheap and expensive ZIP codes are narrowing over time, consistent with capital flowing toward undervalued markets, demand spillovers from expensive neighborhoods into adjacent affordable ones, and supply constraints binding more tightly in already-expensive areas.

The regression specification follows the standard formulation:

\[ \log\!\left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,0}}\right) = \alpha + \beta\,\log(P_{i,0}) + \varepsilon_i \]

where \(P_{i,0}\) is the median home value in ZIP code \(i\) at the start of the study period and \(P_{i,t}\) is the value at the end. A statistically significant negative \(\hat{\beta}\) is evidence of convergence.

Data

Home value data are drawn from the Zillow Home Value Index (ZHVI) at the ZIP-Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) level. This public dataset covers home prices from 2000 until early 2026. However, the dataset has significant gaps. Zillow coverage thins considerably in lower-value, lower-turnover markets for earlier periods. ZIPs with missing initial or terminal values are excluded from each period's regression. My first step was identifiying what zipcodes were missing across the data and determining when the dataset was complete enough for meaningful results.

I found the number of percent of missing ZIP-Codes for each year (out of the 200 in the Kansas City MSA).

These four study periods are identiifed for stastical and economic reasons. Statistically, 2012 was the first year where over 90 percent of zipcodes were present; 2018 had 95 percent, and 2020 had 98 percent. Economically, each period also represents a distinct macroeconomic regime:

  • 2001–2026: Full cycle, covering the dot-com aftermath, subprime boom, Financial Crisis, recovery, and COVID.
  • 2012–2026: Financial Crisis recovery through pandemic; secular appreciation cycle.
  • 2018–2026: Late-cycle through pandemic; captures the full interest rate-environment shift.
  • 2020–2026: Pandemic era; most ZIPs with complete data, strongest convergence signal.

Sigma Convergence

What It Is and Why It Matters

Sigma convergence (σ-convergence) measures whether the cross-sectional dispersion of home values across ZIP codes is declining over time. Where beta convergence tests the rate relationship between initial price and subsequent growth, sigma convergence directly tracks whether the distribution of prices is compressing. Formally, sigma convergence is present when:

\[ \sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\log P_{i,t} - \overline{\log P_t}\right)^2} \]

is declining over time. Beta convergence is a necessary but not sufficient condition for sigma convergence — a negative \(\hat{\beta}\) implies a tendency toward compression, but cross-sectional shocks can widen dispersion even when the convergence mechanism is operating.

Data

Annual \(\sigma_t\) is computed for each year 2000–2026 using all ZIPs with non-missing Zillow ZHVI values in that year. The full time series is plotted below.

Figure 5. Cross-sectional standard deviation of log home values across KC MSA ZIP codes, 2000–2026. Shading indicates four identified convergence regimes.

Four sub-periods were used to explore Sigma Convergence. Again, these periods seem meaningful statistiaclly and econonomically:

  • 2000–2026: Captures the full data set.
  • 2000–2006: Pre-Financial Crisis expansion — broad appreciation driven by subprime credit expansion. Negative slope, indicating price compression.
  • 2007–2014: Financial Crisis and aftermath — foreclosure crisis differentially compressed lower-tier markets, widening dispersion – positive slope.
  • 2015–2026: Post-recovery through pandemic — sustained compression as demand cascaded from expensive to affordable ZIPs – negative slope, again.

Analysis

Linear time trends are estimated within each sub-period to characterize the direction and pace of dispersion change:

sigma2000_2026 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = sigma_data)
sigma2000_2006 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2000, Year <= 2006))
sigma2007_2014 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2007, Year <= 2014))
sigma2015_2026 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2015))

Limitations

  • Survivorship bias. ZIPs excluded from earlier periods due to missing Zillow coverage are disproportionately low-value and low-turnover markets — the same ZIPs most likely to be driving the convergence result. Estimates for earlier periods likely understate the true rate of convergence.
  • ZCTA vs. ZIP codes. Zillow reports at the ZIP code level; Census boundaries use ZCTAs, which do not always align exactly with USPS ZIP codes. Boundary discrepancies are minor but not zero.
  • OLS assumptions. Standard OLS convergence regressions assume independent errors. Spatial autocorrelation testing (see above) evaluates whether this assumption holds; results should be interpreted accordingly.
  • OLS assumptions, cont.OLS also requires that no there are no ommitted variables. There are obviously spatial and non-spatial variables that will impact appreciation rates. This is strictly a unconditional convergence test. No casual inference should be drawn.
  • Single metro. Findings are specific to the Kansas City MSA and may not generalize to other metro areas with different supply elasticities, demographic compositions, or spatial structures.
