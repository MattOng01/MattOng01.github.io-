Overview

Home prices rarely appreciate uniformly across a metropolitan area. Some neighborhoods consistently outperform others, while lower-priced areas occasionally experience periods of unusually rapid appreciation. Understanding whether these patterns reflect random market fluctuations or a systematic convergence process has important implications for investors, developers, lenders, and policymakers.

One way to evaluate this is through β-convergence, an economic framework that tests whether lower-priced markets tend to appreciate faster than higher-priced markets over time.

The distinction matters beyond the theory. If cheaper ZIP codes are systematically catching up, that has real implications for where affordability pressure is building or easing, where investment capital is likely to flow next, and how urban planners and policymakers should think about equitable growth across a metro area.

Using ZIP-code-level home price data for the Kansas City MSA from 2000–2026, this project finds evidence of both beta and sigma convergence. However, as the sections below show, the strength of that convergence varies considerably depending on the time period examined and isn't immune to reversal during periods of economic stress.

Key Takeaways

1

Lower-priced ZIP-codes converge toward higher-priced ones at roughly 3% per year, a rate that has held stable since 2012.

2

Price dispersion narrowed overall, but reversed between 2007–2014 as the foreclosure crisis hit lower-tier markets hardest.

3

Appreciation clusters geographically (Moran's I = 0.632), so comps should be pulled by radius rather than restricted to a single ZIP-code.

Beta Convergence

What It Is and Why It Matters

Originally developed in the economic growth literature, β-convergence describes the tendency for poorer economies to grow faster than richer ones because of diminishing marginal returns to capital. Applied to housing markets, the same framework predicts that lower-priced neighborhoods may experience faster appreciation than expensive ones, gradually reducing differences in home values.

The regression specification follows the standard formulation:

\[ \log\!\left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,0}}\right) = \alpha + \beta\,\log(P_{i,0}) + \varepsilon_i \]

where \(P_{i,0}\) is the median home value in ZIP code \(i\) at the start of the study period and \(P_{i,t}\) is the value at the end.

Data

Home value data are drawn from the Zillow Home Value Index (ZHVI) at the ZIP-Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) level. Each year’s data point is January’s average home sales. The data was filtered to only include the 200 ZIP-codes in the Kansas City MSA. This public dataset covers home prices from January 2000 until January 2026. However, the dataset has significant gaps. Zillow coverage thins considerably for earlier periods. My first step was identifying what ZIP-codes were missing across the data and, importantly, determining when the dataset was complete enough for meaningful results.

I found the number of missing ZIP-Codes for each year and calculated the percent of missing ZIP-Codes.

The period 2001-2026 includes all possible years but has the most missing data. The first year where over 90 percent of ZIP-codes report data is 2012. Data coverage improves again in 2018 and 2020.

Regression Analysis

Separate OLS regressions are estimated for each study period. Log cumulative growth is regressed on log initial home value:

beta2001_2026 <- lm(growth_2001_2026 ~ log_price_2001, data = beta_data)
beta2012_2026 <- lm(growth_2012_2026 ~ log_price_2012, data = beta_data)
beta2018_2026 <- lm(growth_2018_2026 ~ log_price_2018, data = beta_data)
beta2020_2026 <- lm(growth_2020_2026 ~ log_price_2020, data = beta_data)

The regression results are reported below.

The four beta convergence regressions above span different time horizons — 25, 14, 8, and 6 years — but each regresses cumulative log growth over its full window on initial log price.

This distinction is important because the estimated coefficient,β, from a cumulative-growth regression is not directly interpretable as an annual rate of convergence. Formally,

\[ \beta_T = -(1 - e^{-bT}) \]

where b is the underlying annual convergence speed and T is the length of the study period. Because convergence accumulates over time, the magnitude of β increases mechanically as the observation window becomes longer. Consequently, differences in the raw coefficients may simply reflect differences in window length rather than changes in the underlying convergence process.

To place all four study periods on a common basis, each estimated coefficient was converted to an annualized convergence speed using the standard transformation from the convergence literature (Barro & Sala-i-Martin):

\[ b = -\frac{\ln(1+\beta)}{T} \]

Standard errors for the annualized convergence speeds were obtained using the delta method, with 95% confidence intervals calculated as b ± 1.96·SE(b).

Beta convergence coefficients and annualized convergence speeds by study period. Raw β coefficients reflect cumulative growth over different time horizons and are therefore not directly comparable. Annualized convergence speeds place all estimates on a common annual basis.

The table shows that annualization substantially changes the comparison across study periods. While the raw β coefficients differ considerably because of the varying lengths of the estimation windows, the annualized convergence speeds for the 2012–2026, 2018–2026, and 2020–2026 samples cluster within a narrow range of approximately 2.9–3.3% per year. In contrast, the 2001–2026 sample exhibits a noticeably slower annualized convergence speed of approximately 1.1% per year. Although the post-2012 estimates appear similar, formal statistical testing is required to determine whether these differences exceed sampling variability.

Pairwise tests of equality of annualized convergence speeds. The table reports z-tests comparing the estimated annual convergence rates across study periods.

The pairwise comparisons indicate that the annualized convergence speeds estimated for the 2012–2026, 2018–2026, and 2020–2026 periods are not statistically distinguishable from one another (all p > 0.15). By contrast, each post-2012 estimate differs significantly from the annualized convergence speed estimated for the full 2001–2026 period (all p < 0.001).

Results and Discussion

All four study periods produce statistically significant negative coefficients on initial log home price, providing consistent evidence of beta convergence across every estimation window. After adjusting for differences in window length, the evidence suggests that the annual pace of convergence has remained remarkably stable throughout the post-2012 period. Despite substantial differences in housing market conditions—including the post-Financial Crisis recovery, historically low mortgage rates, inventory shortages, remote-work migration, and the COVID-19 housing boom—the estimated convergence speed remains consistently near 3% per year. Rather than indicating that convergence strengthened or weakened over successive subperiods, the results suggest that the underlying convergence process has been robust across a variety of market environments.

The 2001–2026 estimate presents a different picture, with an annualized convergence speed of approximately 1.1% per year that is significantly lower than each of the post-2012 estimates. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The 2001–2026 regression relies on only 62 ZIP codes with available historical price data, compared with approximately 90% coverage in the later samples (see Limitations). Because these ZIP codes are not randomly distributed across the metropolitan area, the slower estimated convergence speed may reflect sample composition rather than a genuinely different convergence regime. A more complete pre-2012 panel would be required to distinguish between these explanations.

Given this tradeoff between historical coverage and sample representativeness, the 2012–2026 specification was selected as the primary model for the remainder of the analysis. This period captures a coherent post-Financial Crisis housing regime while retaining observations for more than 90% of ZIP codes in the Kansas City metropolitan area.

Spatial Autocorrelation

The statistical test used to calculate beta convergence (OLS regression) depends on the assumption of independent statistical errors across observations. Given that home price appreciation is inherently geographic, this assumption is unlikely to hold: appreciation in one ZIP-code plausibly correlates with appreciation in adjacent ZIP-codes. This may be due to direct spillover (demand or development effects crossing ZIP-code boundaries) or shared unobserved factors (school quality, walkability, amenity access) that themselves cluster spatially.

For example, Shawnee Mission East, a premier public high school, is located in 66208; however, ZIP-code 66207 is still within district boundaries. Families that were drawn to Shawnee Mission East were not limited to just homes in 66208. Because Shawnee Mission East’s location in 66208 may affect home prices in 66207, there could be Spatial Autocorrelation.

Luckily, there is a method to test for and address this violation. Spatial autocorrelation was tested for the primary 2012–2026 specification.

Weights Matrix

First, we assign a weight to each ZIP-code. There are several ways to do this, and the method depends on the type of spatial autocorrelation we suspect. In this case, I expect most of the autocorrelation to be spill-over effects (such as in the Shawnee Mission East example). Therefore, my weight matrix followed a Queen Contiguity pattern. In this type of matrix, the weight assigned to a ZIP-code depends on the number of direct neighboring ZIP-codes it has, and the rows are standardized so that each ZIP's neighbors are weighted equally and sum to one. No islands (zero-neighbor ZIPs) were present. The average ZIP had approximately 5.2 neighbors. The full spatial weights matrix is available in the project repository.

This are the weights for five ZIP-codes: 64108, 64109m 64110, 64111, and 64112. ZIP-code 64112 does not touch 64108, so their weight is 0.

Moran's I

Moran's I was applied to the OLS residuals to test whether the regression residuals from the beta convergence model are spatially clustered. Essentially, whether ZIP-codes that appreciate faster than the model predicts (positive residuals) tend to sit next to other ZIP-codes with positive residuals, and likewise for negative residuals. If residuals are spatially random, Moran's I should be close to zero. If they cluster – high residuals next to high, low next to low – Moran's I will be positive. If they act like a checkerboard, it will be negative.

Formally, Moran's I is calculated as:

\[ I = \frac{N}{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} w_{ij}} \cdot \frac{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} w_{ij}(e_i - \bar{e})(e_j - \bar{e})}{\sum_{i}(e_i - \bar{e})^2} \]

where \(e_i\) is the OLS residual for ZIP-code (i), \(w_{ij}\) is the weight between ZIP (i) and (j) from the Queen contiguity matrix described above, and \(N\) is the number of ZIPs. In plain terms, the numerator asks whether the residuals of neighboring ZIP-codes move together, and the denominator normalizes this by the overall variance of the residuals.

I ran Moran's I on the residuals from the primary 2012–2026 beta convergence regression using the Queen contiguity weights matrix. The result was I = 0.632, with a p-value (< 0.001) well below all conventional significance thresholds. This is a strong positive value, suggesting that residuals are clustering geographically, not scattered randomly across the metro.

Substantively, this confirms the concern raised earlier: ZIP-codes that appreciated faster (or slower) than the beta convergence model predicts are not doing so in isolation – they are not necessarily independent. This is consistent with the Shawnee Mission East example: school district boundaries and other neighborhood amenities that affect home value don't follow ZIP-code lines, so their effects on price bleed into adjacent areas. Spatial dependence violates the independence assumption underlying conventional OLS inference. Coefficient estimates remain consistent under exogeneity, but estimated standard errors become unreliable.

Lagrange Multiplier Diagnostics

Moran's I told us the OLS residuals are spatially clustered, but it doesn't tell us why. Spatial dependence can enter a regression in two structurally different ways, and mistaking one for the other leads to the wrong fix. Lagrange Multiplier (LM) tests are designed to distinguish between them.

The first possibility is a spatial lag process: the outcome itself directly depends on appreciation in neighboring ZIP-codes, independent of any other factor. This would be the case if, say, price growth in one ZIP-code directly bid up demand in the ZIP-code next door, through something like a spillover in buyer search behavior.

The second possibility is a spatial error process: the clustering isn't in the outcome itself, but in something left out of the model that happens to be spatially correlated. The Shawnee Mission East–style effects don't appreciate ZIP-codes directly; they're just factors that (a) affect price and (b) cluster geographically. If the model omits them, the omission shows up as correlated error terms rather than a direct lag effect.

This distinction matters because the two diagnoses call for different models. A spatial lag problem is corrected with a Spatial Lag Model (which adds a spatially lagged dependent variable as a regressor). A spatial error problem is corrected with a Spatial Error Model (which models the error term itself as spatially autocorrelated, consistent under the assumption that the omitted spatial factors are uncorrelated with initial price).

How the test works

I used the standard (non-robust) Lagrange Multiplier tests (LM-lag and LM-error). Each test for one form of dependence, but they cross-contaminate: a true spatial error process will often trip the LM-lag test too, and vice versa, because the two forms of dependence are correlated with each other in most real spatial data. Running only the standard tests risks picking the wrong model.

I used the robust Lagrange Multiplier tests (Anselin et al.). These test for each form of dependence (LM-lag and LM-error). What is beneficial here is that the robust form of the test identifies correlation between the lag and error factors and removes them before testing. Robust LM-lag tests for a lag effect after controlling for the possibility of error dependence, and robust LM-error does the reverse. The decision rule is:

  • If both standard tests are significant, look at the robust versions.
  • Whichever robust test remains significant (and the other doesn't) identifies the preferred model.
  • If both robust tests are significant, the higher-magnitude test statistic wins.
  • Recent spatial research (DRS Saputro et al.) has a way to correct for lag and error dependence when they are both present, and this is more robust than treating only the higher-magnitude test; however, the underlying math is much more complex.

What I found

lm_tests <- lm.LMtests(beta_model, lw_queen_2012, test = "all", zero.policy = TRUE)

Running all four tests on the 2012–2026 beta convergence residuals, I found that both the classic Error and Lag models were statistically significant; however, once running the robust test (which controls for correlation between the lag and errors), only the Error model produced a statistically significant result. This indicates that the spatial dependence identified by Moran's I is better characterized as an omitted, spatially-correlated factor than as direct spillover in the outcome itself. This points to the Spatial Error Model as the appropriate correction, which is estimated in the next section.

Spatial Error Model

The robust LM-error test identified the omitted-variable form of spatial dependence as the preferred specification, so the Spatial Error Model (SEM) is estimated as the correction. The SEM keeps the same regression structure as the original beta convergence model, but adds a term that explicitly models spatial autocorrelation in the error:

\[ \log\!\left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,0}}\right) = \alpha + \beta\,\log(P_{i,0}) + u_i, \quad u_i = \lambda \sum_{j} w_{ij}u_j + \varepsilon_i \]

Here, (λ) captures how strongly the error in one ZIP-code is tied to errors in its neighbors, using the same Queen contiguity weights matrix from the Moran's I and LM steps. If \(\lambda\) is close to zero, spatial structure isn't doing much work and OLS was fine all along. If (λ) is large, it suggests omitted spatially correlated factors may be exerting a real pull on the residuals, and OLS standard errors can't be trusted.

error_model_2012 <- errorsarlm(growth_2012_2026 ~ log_price_2012, 
                               data = spatial_df_2012, 
                               listw = lw_queen_2012, 
                               zero.policy = TRUE)

Estimating the SEM on the 2012–2026 data returns (λ = 0.533) (p < 0.001), confirming that spatial dependence in the residuals is both real and substantial — consistent with the Moran's I result. The coefficient on initial log price is β = −0.361 (p < 0.001), close to but not identical to the original OLS estimate of β = −0.368. Convergence still holds under the spatially corrected model, and its magnitude is essentially unchanged, but the standard errors are now valid, since the model no longer assumes the ZIP-codes are statistically independent of one another.

The likelihood ratio test comparing the SEM to OLS is strongly significant (LR = 34.91, p < 0.001), and AIC falls from -234.54 under OLS to -267.45 under the SEM — both indicating the spatial error specification is a meaningfully better fit, not just a technical correction.

Takeaway: accounting for spatial autocorrelation does not overturn the beta convergence finding. Lower-priced ZIP-codes are growing faster than expensive ones at essentially the same rate, but it does mean the original OLS model was understating the uncertainty around that estimate. The convergence result survives the correction; the confidence in the precision of that result did not, until now.

Sigma Convergence

What It Is and Why It Matters

Sigma convergence (σ-convergence) measures whether the cross-sectional dispersion, or the range of home values across ZIP codes is declining over time. Where beta convergence tests the rate relationship between initial price and subsequent growth, sigma convergence directly tracks whether the distribution of prices is compressing. Formally, sigma convergence is present when:

\[ \sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\log P_{i,t} - \overline{\log P_t}\right)^2} \]

is declining over time. Beta convergence is a necessary but not sufficient condition for sigma convergence.

Data

The annual standard deviation of log home price is computed for each year 2000–2026 using all ZIPs with non-missing Zillow ZHVI values in that year. The full time series is plotted below.

Figure 5. Cross-sectional standard deviation of log home values across KC MSA ZIP codes, 2000–2026. Shading indicates four identified convergence regimes.

Again, these sub-periods are economically significant:

  • 2000–2026: The full model.
  • 2000-2006:Pre-Financial Crisis expansion, where broad appreciation was driven by subprime credit expansion. Negative slope, indicating price compression.
  • 2007-2014:The Financial Crisis and aftermath. Foreclosure crisis differentially compressed lower-tier markets, widening dispersion – positive slope.
  • 2015-2026:Post-recovery through the Pandemic. Sustained compression as demand shifted from expensive to affordable ZIPs – negative slope, again.

Analysis

Linear time trends are estimated within each sub-period to characterize the direction and pace of dispersion change:

sigma2000_2026 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = sigma_data)
sigma2000_2006 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2000, Year <= 2006))
sigma2007_2014 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2007, Year <= 2014))
sigma2015_2026 <- lm(sigma ~ Year, data = filter(sigma_data, Year >= 2015))

Figure 6. Cross-sectional dispersion with fitted linear trends overlaid for each sub-period. Positive slope = divergence; negative slope = sigma convergence.

Discussion

The sigma convergence results tell a more cyclical story than beta convergence alone would suggest. The range of log home values compressed steadily during the pre-Financial Crisis expansion (2000–2006), consistent with broad-based appreciation lifting lower-value ZIPs alongside higher-value ones.

This compression reversed sharply during the Financial Crisis and its aftermath (2007–2014), when dispersion widened. This is consistent with the foreclosure crisis disproportionately depressing lower-income markets rather than affecting all ZIP-codes uniformly, temporarily undoing prior convergence gains. From 2015 onward, dispersion resumed its decline, consistent with the strong housing market and beta convergence signal identified in the 2012–2026 regression.

This regime-dependent pattern is a useful complement to the beta convergence findings: it demonstrates that convergence is not a constant process, but can instead pause or reverse during periods of asymmetric shock. It also reinforces that beta convergence alone is an incomplete test. While a negative beta coefficient indicates a tendency toward compression on average, the sigma convergence series shows this tendency was actively overridden during 2007–2014, precisely the kind of divergence episode beta convergence alone cannot capture.

Conclusions

Real Estate

For an investor or advisory context, the practical read is that lower-priced ZIP-codes experienced faster appreciation, and sigma convergence indicates that this faster growth coincided with a reduction in price dispersion over most of the sample. The spatial autocorrelation results reinforce that this appreciation isn't ZIP-code-specific; it clusters geographically, meaning the relevant unit of analysis for identifying "catching up" markets is closer to a corridor or district than an individual ZIP-code.

The spatial autocorrelation results are also consistent with the standard practice of pulling comps within an x-mile radius of a subject property, rather than restricting a primary market area to a single ZIP-code. Appreciation clusters geographically, so a radius-based approach captures the spillover effects a ZIP-bounded comp set would miss.

Economic

The evidence across both beta and sigma convergence, and now confirmed under the spatially corrected model, supports a real but conditional convergence process in the Kansas City MSA. Lower-priced ZIP-codes are growing faster than expensive ones (β = −0.361 under the spatial error model), and the cross-sectional spread in prices is narrowing over most of the sample period. But the sigma convergence results show this isn't a smooth, mechanical process; it reversed during 2007–2014, when the Financial Crisis hit lower-tier markets hardest and temporarily widened the gap it had spent the prior decade closing. Convergence in housing markets, like in Solow's original growth framework, holds on average but is vulnerable to asymmetric economic shocks.

Policy

The convergence finding is consistent with a metro area where home prices in lower-priced ZIP-codes are rising. But the 2007–2014 reversal is a caution against reading convergence as inevitable: it can be undone by external shocks that hit lower-value housing stock hardest, such as foreclosure waves or credit tightening. This suggests convergence should be treated as a trend that policy can support or undercut, not a background process planners can assume will simply continue. The spatial clustering also highlights the importance of place-based investments, such as transit, schools, or amenities that will plausibly spill into adjacent ZIP-codes rather than targeting single ZIP-codes in isolation.

Limitations

  • Sample bias. ZIPs excluded from earlier periods due to missing Zillow coverage are disproportionately low-value and low-turnover markets — the same ZIPs most likely to be driving the convergence result. Estimates for earlier periods likely understate the true rate of convergence.
  • ZCTA vs. ZIP codes. Zillow reports at the ZIP code level; Census boundaries use ZCTAs, which do not always align exactly with USPS ZIP codes. Boundary discrepancies are minor but not zero.
  • OLS assumptions. Standard OLS convergence regressions assume independent errors. Spatial autocorrelation testing (see above) evaluates whether this assumption holds; results should be interpreted accordingly.
  • OLS assumptions, cont.OLS also requires that there be no omitted variables. There are obviously spatial and non-spatial variables that will impact appreciation rates. This is strictly an unconditional convergence test. No causal inference should be drawn.
  • Bias.The primary model still has missing data. Although 92% of ZIP-codes have data for the 2012-2026 period, the missing data may not be random and correlate to under-priced ZIP-codes.
  • Time UnitsI chose January as the representative data point for each year. This was arbitrary as data for each month was available (up to June 2026).
  • Single metro. Findings are specific to the Kansas City MSA and may not generalize to other metro areas with different supply elasticities, demographic compositions, or spatial structures.

Next Steps

This analysis is strictly an unconditional convergence test. It shows that convergence is happening but not why. A natural next step would be to find conditional convergence by identifying covariates that explain appreciation directly and running them through the spatial errors framework. These variables could include school quality, transit access, grocery store access, and new construction activity. Adding these would improve the model’s explanatory power and begin to untangle which omitted factors are driving the spatial error clustering.

The sigma convergence periods were chosen visually. This is not necessarily wrong, but there is a formal test to identify break points (the Bai-Perron test). Future work would incorporate that.

This analysis relies on a single observation per year (January ZHVI), rather than the full monthly series available from Zillow. Given that beta and sigma convergence are measured over multi-year windows, within-year seasonal variation in home prices is unlikely to meaningfully affect the estimated convergence coefficients. Testing this directly with monthly observations, potentially using a panel or time-series specification rather than repeated cross-sections, is a natural robustness check for future work
