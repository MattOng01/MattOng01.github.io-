Overview

We often think of neighborhood amenities like parks, walking trails, restaurants and bars, and maybe police, fire, and hospitals. Rarely do we consider grocery stores to be amenities — either because they seem so commonplace or because they are a short car ride away.

Unfortunately, this is not true for everyone in suburban America. Food access remains a very real challenge for many in the Kansas City metro area. This led me to map food access using several different variables to assess where challenges persist.

I invite you to explore the interactive map below.

Key Takeaways

1

Lower-income and higher-poverty areas often overlap with USDA-designated low-income, low-food-access tracts.

2

Grocery store access varies significantly across the county.

3

Several low-income, low-vehicle-ownership tracts are more than a half mile from a grocery store and lack public transit access.

4

Despite these gaps, the bus network provides relatively strong connections to many grocery stores.

5

Troost Avenue remains a useful reference for understanding spatial patterns, particularly in lower-income neighborhoods.

Variable Selection

Reference Roads

Troost Avenue is a major north-south road that connects downtown Kansas City to the suburbs. Troost is also a historic barrier in the metropolitan area dating back to the 1920s. It became associated with Jim Crow-era segregation, as Black and Jewish residents were often unable to purchase property west of Troost, and investment east of Troost lagged behind the rest of the metropolitan area.

US-71 Highway runs through Kansas City parallel to Troost Avenue. This section of US-71 was constructed during the 1980s and 1990s. Roughly 10,000 residents were displaced by its construction.

Other reference roads in Jackson County include the I-435 loop, 63rd Street, and Emanuel Cleaver II Boulevard.

SNAP Grocery Stores

Grocery stores that accept SNAP benefits are highlighted to illustrate food access. This dataset is maintained by the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Because this analysis focuses on 2019, locations that stopped accepting SNAP benefits before January 1, 2020, were excluded. Furthermore, only full-service supermarkets, small, medium, and large grocery stores, and farmers markets were included. This was necessary to exclude gas stations and convenience stores that do not necessarily provide complete and healthy food options.

Bus Routes

Bus route and stop data were obtained from the Kansas City Area Transportation Authority. Although the other variables reflect conditions in 2019, historic route and stop location data were unavailable. Some changes to the transit system likely occurred between 2019 and 2026.

Demographic Variables

The variables selected illustrate the socioeconomic conditions of Jackson County in 2019. There are a few variables worth noting:

  • Below 60% AMI refers to census tracts where the median income is below 60 percent of the Area Median Income (AMI), as defined by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. In 2019, the Kansas City metropolitan area's AMI was $82,700, making 60 percent of AMI equal to $49,620. This threshold is meaningful because it is commonly used to determine eligibility for affordable housing under HUD's Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program.
  • LILA Flag identifies census tracts that meet the USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) criteria for being both low income and low food access. Tracts that meet this designation are commonly referred to as food deserts.

More information about variable selection and processing is available in the README file of the Project Code Repository.
